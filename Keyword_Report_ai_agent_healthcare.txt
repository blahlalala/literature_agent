**Literature Analysis and Research Proposal Report**

**1. Background**  
The integration of autonomous AI agents into clinical decision support systems represents a paradigm shift in healthcare informatics. Driven by advances in large language models (LLMs) and agent-oriented architectures, these systems increasingly perform complex tasks—including real-time EHR analysis, diagnostic hypothesis generation, and inter-departmental coordination. However, their deployment faces profound challenges rooted in safety, trust, regulatory compliance, and interoperability. As healthcare transitions from static AI models to dynamic, agentic workflows, foundational questions about governance, accountability, and human–machine collaboration remain unresolved. This report synthesizes recent scholarly findings to clarify the current state of research and identify high-impact, actionable directions for future work.

**2. Related Work**  
Recent literature establishes several key pillars for responsible agentic AI in healthcare. First, zero-trust security architectures are now recognized as essential—not optional—for mitigating risks arising from shell execution, database access, and multi-party communication among agents [1]. Second, human-in-the-loop governance is gaining empirical traction, with studies underscoring clinician guidance as critical to aligning automated data science with clinical reasoning and workflow constraints [2]. Third, privacy-by-design and multilingual capability are no longer peripheral features but foundational requirements for global deployability, particularly in settings with heterogeneous regulatory regimes and linguistic diversity [4]. Fourth, formalized lifecycle management frameworks are emerging to address operational scalability—covering version control, audit trails, and systematic deprecation of agents across departments [3]. Finally, multi-agent collusion has been newly identified as a systemic threat: adversarial consensus methods demonstrate how LLM-based agents can coordinate maliciously without oversight, undermining integrity and safety [5].

**3. Research Trends**  
Five convergent trends define the current research landscape. (i) Security paradigms are shifting from perimeter-based to zero-trust models, explicitly designed for agent autonomy and distributed execution. (ii) Governance is evolving toward human-centered, iterative oversight rather than static policy enforcement. (iii) Privacy and localization are being embedded at the architectural level—not retrofitted—through standards-aligned design (e.g., FHIR, ISO/IEC 27001). (iv) Operational maturity is being prioritized via structured lifecycle protocols that enable traceability and regulatory compliance. (v) Threat modeling is expanding beyond single-agent vulnerabilities to include emergent, system-level risks such as adversarial consensus across agent coalitions.

**4. Research Gaps**  
Despite progress, three critical gaps persist. First, *evaluation infrastructure* remains inadequate: no standardized benchmark exists for assessing human-guided agent performance in real-world clinical prediction. Current benchmarks (e.g., AgentDS) lack integration with live EHR systems and fail to incorporate clinician feedback loops—rendering them insufficient for measuring clinical utility, safety latency, or trust [2]. Second, *governance implementation* is underdeveloped: while conceptual frameworks abound, there is a complete absence of empirical studies on policy enforcement, accountability attribution, or auditability of agent actions within production healthcare environments [3]. Third, *interoperability standards* are missing: no existing work defines cross-agent interoperability protocols—particularly for Model Context Protocol (MCP) agents—across heterogeneous hospital IT infrastructures, severely limiting reproducibility, regulatory validation, and clinical adoption [4].

**5. Research Directions**  
To bridge these gaps, we propose five empirically grounded, technically feasible research directions. (i) Develop an open, EHR-integrated benchmark for human-guided agentic AI that evaluates clinical utility, safety latency, and clinician trust—not just predictive accuracy—using real-time feedback from frontline providers [2]. (ii) Design and validate a lightweight, auditable agent lifecycle ledger using blockchain-inspired immutable logging to enforce consent-aware data access and role-based action approval in regulated healthcare settings [3]. (iii) Propose and test a privacy-preserving multi-agent coordination protocol—e.g., differential privacy–augmented voting or zero-knowledge agent attestation—to prevent adversarial consensus while preserving collaborative diagnostic reasoning [5]. (iv) Build and evaluate a multilingual, FHIR-compliant MCP agent adapter enabling plug-and-play deployment across national health IT ecosystems (e.g., NHS Digital, Germany’s ePA, Japan’s My Number Health Portal) [4]. (v) Investigate “agent red-teaming” methodologies specifically for healthcare: systematic, clinically grounded penetration testing of zero-trust architectures against realistic threat models—including prompt injection combined with privilege escalation chains [1]. Collectively, these directions prioritize empirical rigor, regulatory alignment, and clinical co-design—ensuring that agentic AI advances not only technical capability but also patient safety, equity, and trust.
